What this sheet tests: grammatical correctness and fit. In each question: one option is genuinely ungrammatical (red), one is grammatical but a worse fit (orange), and one matches the meaning the sentence is after (green).
Core idea: English signals time with the verb. Past simple = finished action at a finished time. Past continuous = action in progress around that time. Past perfect = one past action happened before another. Will / going to = future plans. Will have + past participle = finished by a future deadline.

1. The situation (read first)

Mark is a backend developer. He could not keep working on the auth system until the external provider fixed a login bug. The fix took the provider two weeks and came through on a Tuesday. He then continued, but the team had already lost two weeks. Next sprint they are going to add biometric authentication. By the end of Q3 they will have shipped it.

2. Past simple — finished action, finished time

UseExampleSignal words
A completed action in the pastThe provider fixed the bug on Tuesday.yesterday, last week, on Tuesday
A sequence of finished actionsHe stopped, waited, then resumed.then, after that, finally
A finished stateMark couldn't keep working.until, when

3. Past continuous — action in progress

UseExampleSignal words
Action in progress at a past momentMark was working on the auth system at 10 a.m.at 10 a.m., all morning, while
Background action interrupted by a shorter oneHe was debugging when the provider called.when + past simple
Two actions happening at the same timeShe was testing while he was writing docs.while, as
Your example: "Mark couldn't keep working until the provider fixed it" is past simple — a finished state with a clear endpoint. If you say "Mark was working when the provider fixed the bug," you describe the action in progress at the moment of interruption. Both are correct; the choice depends on focus.

4. Past perfect — the "before that" tense

UseExampleSignal words
An action finished before another past actionThe provider had fixed the bug before Mark resumed.before, after, already, by the time
A past result visible at a past momentThe team had lost two weeks by Tuesday.by Tuesday, by then
Reported speech / regretHe said he hadn't known about the bug.said, realised, wished
Rule of thumb: past perfect is the safe choice when you want the "earlier" action unmistakable. With "before" / "after" in the sentence, past simple often works too — but past perfect makes the order explicit.
